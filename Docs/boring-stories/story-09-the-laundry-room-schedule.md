# The Laundry Room Schedule

The apartment building laundry room was on the basement level, between the storage lockers and the door to the underground parking garage. It had two washers, two dryers, a folding table, a utility sink, and a noticeboard with a schedule clipped behind a clear plastic cover. The room was painted light gray. The floor was speckled tile, easy to sweep and difficult to make look completely new.

The schedule was divided by apartment number. Some residents preferred mornings. Some preferred evenings. Some did not use their assigned times and came only when the machines were free. The building manager updated the sheet every season, not because the schedule changed often, but because the paper became wrinkled from people lifting the plastic cover to check it more closely.

On Monday morning, the manager brought a fresh schedule downstairs. It had been printed in the office on the main floor. The old schedule was removed first. Two magnets held the plastic cover in place. The magnets were shaped like small blue squares and had faded along the top edges. The manager placed them on the folding table, lifted the cover, took out the old sheet, and slid in the new one.

The first washer had a small scratch near the coin slot, though the machines no longer used coins. They had been converted to card readers, and the coin slots remained only because replacing the panels would have cost more than leaving them. The card reader on washer one blinked green. Washer two showed a steady blue light. Dryer one was empty. Dryer two held a lint screen that had been cleaned but not pushed all the way back into place.

The manager pushed the lint screen in until it clicked. Then the top of each machine was wiped with a cloth. Detergent dust collected there, especially near the washers. Sometimes a small ring from a bottle cap appeared. Sometimes a single dryer sheet rested behind the machines where it had fallen and flattened itself against the wall. Today there was no dryer sheet, only a button under the folding table. The button was white with four holes. It went into the lost-and-found jar.

The lost-and-found jar sat on the shelf above the sink. It contained buttons, a child-sized sock, two collar stays, one key with no label, and several coins from before the card readers were installed. A note on the jar said "small laundry items." The note had been written by a previous manager, whose handwriting was narrow and slanted. No one had claimed the key, though it had been there for at least a year.

At nine, apartment 203 arrived with a plastic basket. The resident checked the schedule, nodded, and loaded washer one. Towels first, then socks, then a few pale shirts. Detergent was measured from a blue cap and poured into the drawer. The drawer closed smoothly. The card reader beeped once. Water began to run, first loudly, then more quietly as the drum filled.

The laundry room had a particular rhythm when one washer was running. Water, pause, turn, pause, water again. The sound traveled into the hallway but not far. Behind the storage lockers, the building pipes made occasional soft clicks. The manager stood by the utility sink and checked the supply cabinet. Paper towels: one full roll, one partial. Floor cleaner: half bottle. Spare schedule sheets: two. Light bulbs: three.

The folding table was laminated white with metal legs. Its front right leg had a rubber foot that was newer than the others. The old foot had split and been replaced. The table was useful not only for folding, but also for sorting mail when the lobby table was being polished, for holding plants during hallway cleaning, and for laying out notices before they were posted. Today it held only the two blue magnets and the old schedule sheet, which the manager folded once and placed in the recycling bag.

At ten, washer one finished. It played a short tune, five notes, then unlocked. Apartment 203 moved the towels to dryer one and wiped the washer gasket with a small cloth. This was appreciated but not required. The dryer door closed with a hollow sound. The card reader beeped again. Dryer one began to turn, and the room became warmer.

Washer two remained unused until eleven-fifteen, when apartment 408 came down with a canvas hamper. Apartment 408 always checked pockets carefully. A receipt, a hair tie, and a folded grocery list were placed on the folding table before the clothes went in. The grocery list had bananas, dish soap, rice, and stamps written on it. After a moment, the resident put it back into a pocket of the hamper.

The manager returned at noon to mop. The machines were in use, so only the open part of the floor could be done. The mop bucket had a wringer that squeaked when pressed. The floor darkened in a clean arc, then dried slowly from the edges inward. A wet floor sign was placed near the door. The sign had a folding hinge and a small mark where someone had once stepped on it.

In the afternoon, the laundry room was empty for nearly two hours. Dryer one cooled. Washer two finished and was unloaded. The utility sink dripped once every few minutes until the manager tightened the handle. The new schedule stayed flat under its cover. The blue magnets held the corners. A small draft came under the parking garage door whenever it opened, bringing a cooler smell of concrete and tires, then fading.

At three, apartment 112 used both washers, which was allowed during open hours. The resident sorted dark clothes into washer one and bedding into washer two. The bedding filled the drum more fully, so the resident removed one pillowcase and put it with the dark clothes instead. The washer doors locked. The two machines began at slightly different times, creating overlapping cycles that settled into a steady pattern.

The noticeboard beside the schedule also held reminders. Please clean lint screen. Do not leave laundry overnight. Report leaks to management. Lost items in jar. The reminders were laminated and had rounded corners. One corner of the lint-screen reminder had lifted from the board, so the manager added a fresh piece of tape at four-thirty. The tape was clear and nearly invisible except where it crossed the edge of the laminate.

By evening, the room had been used but not disturbed. The machines showed ordinary wear. The folding table was clean. The lost-and-found jar had one new button. The schedule was current. The dryer lint screens were empty. The manager turned off the overhead light, leaving only the small glow from the card readers, green and blue in the dim room. Upstairs, laundry would return to drawers and shelves, and downstairs the schedule would wait behind its plastic cover for Tuesday.

On Tuesday morning, the room began again with different apartments and the same machines. The manager did not need to replace the schedule, but checked it anyway. The plastic cover had stayed in place. The magnets had not moved. Apartment 305 had the first slot, though no one arrived at first. At nine-ten, washer one and washer two were still empty, doors slightly open to let air move through the drums.

The manager used the quiet time to clean the soap drawers. Each drawer slid out after a small tab was pressed. Detergent had collected in the corners, pale blue in one drawer and white in the other. The drawers were rinsed in the utility sink with warm water and dried with a paper towel. The compartments were labeled wash, softener, bleach. The labels were molded into the plastic and could not wear off.

Behind the machines, the floor was harder to reach. A long-handled duster was used there once a week. It brought out lint, two twist ties, and a flat square of cardboard from a detergent box. The cardboard had softened at one edge. It was placed in the recycling bag. The lint went into the trash. The twist ties went into the lost-and-found jar for a moment, then were removed, because the jar was not for twist ties.

At ten, apartment 305 arrived after all, apologizing to no one in particular for being late. The schedule allowed enough flexibility. Clothes went into washer one. A striped towel was added last, then removed, then added again after the resident considered the color. The washer began. The manager wrote "soap drawers cleaned" on the maintenance sheet clipped inside the supply cabinet.

The building's laundry room did not require much from anyone at once. It asked only that lint screens be cleared, doors be left open when machines were empty, spills be wiped, and the schedule be treated as a guide rather than a contest. The machines did their turning and rinsing. The table held baskets. The jar held small lost things. The posted notices stayed where they were. In this way, the room served the building through repeated, ordinary cycles.
