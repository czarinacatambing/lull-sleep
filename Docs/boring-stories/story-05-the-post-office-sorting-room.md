# The Post Office Sorting Room

Behind the public counter of the small post office was a sorting room with no sign on the door. Customers could see only a narrow part of it when parcels were carried through, just a strip of gray floor, a wall of pigeonholes, and the edge of a rolling hamper. The room was larger than it appeared from the lobby. It extended behind the counter and then turned slightly to the left, where the delivery cases stood in two long rows.

The day began with canvas sacks. They arrived on a metal cart, each sack tied with a plastic seal and marked with a route code. The morning clerk checked the codes against a printed sheet. Then the seals were cut and placed in a small container so they would not fall on the floor. The sacks were opened one at a time. Letters came first, bundled with rubber bands. Then flats, which meant larger envelopes and magazines. Then small parcels, padded mailers, and the occasional oddly shaped package that had to be set aside and considered separately.

The letter sorting case had many narrow slots. Each slot represented an address range, a street, a rural box, or a special category. The labels above the slots were printed in black on white strips. Some labels had been replaced recently and were bright. Others had yellowed slightly with age. There was a slot for forwarding, a slot for holds, a slot for mis-sorts, and a slot for items that needed postage due.

The clerk began with the first bundle. The letters made a soft, dry sound as they were separated. Window envelope. Handwritten envelope. Utility notice. Bank statement. Greeting card. Catalog postcard. Each item moved from the clerk's left hand to the correct slot. The motion was steady, not fast. The point was accuracy. If a letter for Pine Street went into the Oak Street slot, it would travel farther than needed and return later with a small mark on it.

The flats were sorted on a wider table. Magazines lay in uneven stacks. Some had glossy covers with bright photographs. Some were thin newsletters folded in half. One envelope contained legal documents and had a stiff cardboard backing. Another was marked "do not bend," though it already had a slight curve from the sack. The clerk placed it under a heavier flat for a few minutes to encourage it back toward straightness.

At the parcel station, a handheld scanner sat in its charging cradle. The screen lit when lifted. Each parcel was scanned, weighed if necessary, and placed by route. The scanner made a small confirming sound after each barcode. Most labels scanned easily. A few required the parcel to be tilted away from the overhead light. One label had a wrinkle through the barcode, so the number was typed by hand.

The sorting room had a clock above the main case. It was five minutes fast, intentionally or not. Everyone knew this and used it only as a reminder. The official time came from the computer at the counter. Still, the wall clock was useful because it was visible from everywhere in the room. At seven fifty, by the fast clock, the carriers began arriving.

Each carrier had a case. The cases looked similar but were arranged differently, because every route had its own order. Streets folded into cul-de-sacs, cul-de-sacs into apartment buildings, apartment buildings into business stops, and business stops back into residential blocks. The carriers sorted the mail into walking or driving sequence, placing letters upright, flats behind them, and small parcels in tubs below.

Route 3 had many apartment mailboxes. Route 5 had more businesses. Route 7 had the longest rural section, with boxes spaced far apart along two roads outside town. The carrier for Route 7 kept a separate list of parcel lockers and large packages that would not fit in roadside boxes. The list was laminated, but a paper note was clipped to it for temporary holds.

Temporary holds were kept in a shallow tray near the supervisor's desk. Each hold had a start date and an end date. Some were for vacations. Some were for moves that were not yet final. Some were for people who preferred to pick up mail because a mailbox had been damaged. The tray was checked each morning. If a hold ended that day, the mail was bundled, marked, and returned to the route.

At eight twenty, a customer knocked at the side service window to ask about a package notice. The window was used before lobby hours for certain pickups. The clerk found the package on the shelf marked "notices left." It was a rectangular box with a bookstore return address and a corner slightly softened from handling. The customer signed the small screen with a finger. The signature looked like a short wave. The scanner accepted it.

The sorting continued. A postcard from a dentist. A seed catalog. Three envelopes from the same insurance company. A thick packet from the county office. A birthday card with a raised sticker on the front. A letter with no apartment number went to the address correction slot. A parcel with an incomplete label went to the supervisor's table, where a directory and computer search might solve it later.

The room's sounds stayed practical. Paper sliding. Scanner beeping. Cart wheels turning. Tape being pressed down on a repaired parcel seam. The distant bell from the lobby door, though the lobby was not officially open yet. Someone set down a tub a little too firmly, then moved it back two inches so the walkway was clear.

Near nine, the carriers began pulling down their routes. This meant taking mail from the case in order and bundling it for delivery. Rubber bands were stretched around groups of letters. Flats were placed behind. Parcels were loaded into hampers according to the sequence of the route. The supervisor checked that outgoing mail had been separated from incoming mail and that the blue collection bags were ready.

One hamper had a label holder that stuck. The carrier pressed it once, then again, then slid the paper route label into place from the other side. The holder accepted it at last. Another carrier replaced a broken rubber band with one from a box near the case. The broken band went into the trash, where it rested on top of a strip of backing paper from adhesive labels.

By nine-thirty, the main sorting was done. The lobby had opened. The first line of customers formed and dissolved in the usual way. In the sorting room, the tables were cleared. Empty sacks were folded and stacked. The scanner returned to its cradle. The clock above the case still showed five minutes ahead.

After the carriers left, the room seemed larger. The pigeonholes were not empty, but they were calmer. A few held forwarded mail. A few held holds. One held the letter with no apartment number, waiting for research. The rolling hamper stood by the wall with its wheels straight. The morning's work had moved outward into town, slot by slot, bundle by bundle, address by address, leaving behind only the quiet order that would be used again tomorrow.

There was still the outgoing mail to prepare. It collected through the morning in two places: the slot in the lobby wall and the blue box outside near the curb. At ten, the clerk unlocked the lobby slot from behind and lifted out the tray. Letters lay face up and face down, some stamped, some metered, some with labels printed at home. The clerk turned them all the same way, checking that postage appeared in the upper right corner and that return addresses, when present, were readable.

The blue box outside required a different key. The clerk put on a reflective vest, though the box was only a few steps from the door. The curb lane could be busy. The collection door opened downward, and the mail slid into a canvas bag inside. The clerk removed the bag, tied it, replaced it with an empty one, and checked that the pickup time label on the box was still clear. The label said weekdays, 4:30. The plastic cover over the label had a scratch across it, but the time was easy to read.

Back inside, outgoing letters were faced, bundled, and placed in a tray by destination category. Local. Regional. Out of area. International. The international mail was a small stack: two postcards, one square envelope, and a padded mailer with a customs form. The customs form had been filled out completely, including weight and contents. The clerk placed it in the correct tray.

Returned mail had its own process. One envelope came back marked moved, no forwarding address. Another had an address that did not exist. A third had been torn by machinery and sealed in a clear apology sleeve. The clerk entered each reason into the system, applied the necessary marks, and placed the items in a tub for return handling. None of this was dramatic. It was simply how mail found its way forward or back.

At the supervisor's table, the parcel with the incomplete label was solved. The street number had printed clearly, but the street name had faded where the label crossed a seam. The supervisor compared the sender's receipt number, the visible postal code, and the route directory. There were only two possible streets, and one did not have that number. The missing name was written on a correction label and placed carefully above the barcode without covering it.

The address-correction letter also found its answer. It belonged to an apartment building where the sender had omitted the unit number. The clerk checked the building directory from a previous delivery note and found a likely match. Because likely was not enough, the letter was held for carrier confirmation. It went into a small sleeve marked ask Route 5. Route 5 would return in the afternoon and know whether the name matched the building.

By late morning, the sorting room had entered its second rhythm. Less movement, more checking. The counter bell rang in the lobby. The printer produced labels. A tub was carried from one table to another. The wall clock continued to be five minutes fast. The sacks from the morning delivery were folded flat and stacked by size, their fabric creased from long use. The room waited for the afternoon collection, when another round of paper and parcels would arrive to be sorted into another set of places.
