# The Small Museum Log

The museum opened at ten, but the building had already been awake for some time. At eight forty-five, the side door clicked open, and the morning attendant stepped into the staff corridor with a ring of keys, a canvas bag, and a folded list from the previous afternoon. The corridor smelled faintly of floor polish and paper. The light switches were arranged in three rows near the door, each labeled with a small strip from a label maker. Gallery one. Gallery two. Office. Hallway. Storage. Cafe. The cafe light switch did not control the kitchen lights, only the small pendant lamps above the counter.

The attendant began with gallery one. It was the local history room, though the sign above the doorway called it the Settlement Gallery. The room contained a long glass case with farm tools, school ledgers, a brass bell, and a pair of wire spectacles. There was also a model of the old railway station, made from painted wood. The platform on the model had four tiny benches, each no longer than a finger. One bench had come loose the previous year and had been glued back down at a slight angle. The angle was recorded in the condition log and judged acceptable.

The humidity reader in gallery one showed forty-six percent. The attendant wrote this in the logbook, using the black pen attached to the clipboard by a cord. The cord had a knot near the end where it had once frayed. Temperature: twenty-one degrees. Lights: normal. Cases: locked. Floor: clear. Notes: none.

In gallery two, the textiles were kept under lower light. Quilts hung behind a protective rail, their colors gentle and a little faded. Each quilt had a small card with the maker's name if the maker was known, and "maker unknown" if not. The cards were printed in the same font and placed at the same height. One card had shifted slightly in its holder, leaving the top edge not quite parallel with the shelf below. The attendant straightened it with two fingers, then stepped back to check the line.

The log for gallery two required a separate note about moth traps. There were three of them, all small, all tucked where visitors would not notice. Trap A under the quilt case. Trap B behind the spinning wheel. Trap C inside the storage alcove. Each trap was checked on Mondays. This was Wednesday, so the attendant only confirmed that they were still in place.

Gallery three was the natural history corner, though it was not large enough to be called a full gallery. It held a display of local stones, a cabinet of pressed plants, and a case containing bird nests that had been collected long ago. The stones were arranged by type, with labels that included both common names and longer geological names. The granite sample was speckled black, white, and pink. The shale sample split into layers. The quartz sample caught the light along one edge. A child had once asked if the quartz was made of sugar. The question was not in the official log, but the attendant remembered it.

The pressed plants were stored in shallow drawers. Only the top drawer was open for display. The plants lay on cream paper, held by thin strips. A label beside each specimen gave the date and place where it had been collected. Some of the place names were still used. Others referred to farms, lanes, or small crossings that no longer appeared on maps.

After the galleries, the attendant checked the front desk. Brochures were arranged in three stacks: museum map, membership form, walking tour. The membership forms were the shortest stack, so five more were added from the cupboard. The donation box was empty except for two coins, which had been left after closing and recorded in yesterday's cash sheet. The visitor counter was reset to zero. The stamp pad for children's activity sheets was opened, inspected, and closed again. The ink was still dark enough.

At nine-thirty, the curator arrived and placed a thermos on the office table. The curator asked whether the humidity had changed in gallery two. It had not. The attendant said forty-eight percent, same as Tuesday. The curator nodded and made a note on a yellow sticky pad. The sticky pad already held three other notes: call printer, replace bulb by cabinet, check ladder.

The bulb by the cabinet was in the hallway outside the temporary exhibit room. It had not burned out completely, but it flickered after being on for several minutes. The attendant stood beneath it and looked up. For a while it was steady. Then it made a small hesitation, dimmed, returned, and held. The ladder was needed because the ceiling there was higher than in the galleries. The curator added the task to the maintenance sheet for Friday.

The temporary exhibit was about calendars. It was a quiet exhibit, assembled from wall calendars donated by families in town, office calendars from closed businesses, and a few pocket diaries under glass. There were calendars with landscapes, calendars with recipes, calendars from hardware stores, calendars with kittens, and calendars from insurance agents. One from 1987 had a red circle around June 14 with the words "garage shelves" written in blue ink. No one knew whether the shelves were bought, repaired, painted, or merely discussed.

At ten, the front door was unlocked. The open sign was turned outward. For the first several minutes, no one came in. The museum often began this way. The building settled into its public hours. The HVAC system hummed softly. The cash drawer sat ready with small bills and coins. The guest book lay open to a clean line. A pen rested beside it, parallel with the bottom edge of the page.

The first visitor arrived at ten seventeen. He asked whether the museum still had the old fire engine bell. The attendant said yes, in gallery one, in the case near the railway model. The visitor walked slowly toward it, stopping first at the framed town map and then at the display of school photographs. The attendant marked one visitor on the counter.

At eleven, a school group was expected, but the group was delayed. The curator received a call and wrote "11:20 maybe" on the yellow sticky pad. The attendant used the extra time to restock pencils in the activity baskets. Each basket needed twelve pencils, six clipboards, and twelve folded hunt sheets. The hunt sheets asked visitors to find a tool with a wooden handle, something with a date on it, a pattern made from triangles, and a photograph of a building that was no longer standing.

The day continued in small entries. Light replaced. Postcards counted. Window latch checked. Three visitors asked about the restroom. One visitor bought a walking tour booklet. A child noticed the angled bench on the railway model and asked if it was supposed to be like that. The attendant said it had been repaired and was stable. This seemed to satisfy the child.

At closing, the lights were switched off in reverse order. Cafe. Storage. Hallway. Office. Gallery two. Gallery one. The logbook was returned to the drawer. The donation box was emptied, counted, and recorded. Outside, the sign was turned inward. The side door clicked shut behind the attendant, and the museum returned to its evening condition, with the cases locked, the labels straight, and the small wooden bench in the model station holding its careful angle.

Before the final lockup, there was also the matter of the storage room. The storage room was not part of the visitor route, but it was part of the museum's daily awareness. It held exhibit panels, empty mounts, archival boxes, extra brochure stands, and several objects waiting for catalog numbers. The door opened with a different key from the gallery cases. Inside, the lights came on slowly, one tube after another, until the shelves appeared in rows.

The archival boxes were arranged by accession year. Each box had a number on the front and a contents note, written in pencil on a removable card. The cards were never taped, because tape became a problem later. Box 2016-14 contained correspondence, three photographs, and one program from a concert. Box 2019-03 contained shop ledgers. Box 2022-08 contained fabric samples in tissue. The attendant did not open the boxes, only checked that the numbers were in order and that no lid had shifted.

On the worktable lay a donated object: a metal measuring scoop from a grain elevator. It had been cleaned, photographed, and placed on a foam pad. A temporary tag was tied to the handle with cotton string. The tag included the donor's name, the date received, and a short description. The curator had not yet decided whether it belonged in the agricultural tools section or the business history section. Until then, it waited on the foam pad in a way that seemed patient.

Beside the scoop were three photograph sleeves. The photographs showed the same street corner in different years. In the earliest one, there were horses. In the middle one, there were parked cars with rounded fenders. In the latest, there was a traffic light and a sign for a hardware store. The attendant checked that each photograph was in its sleeve and that no sleeve had been left open. The open edge faced upward, as preferred by the curator.

The extra exhibit panels leaned vertically in a rack. They were covered in cloth sleeves to keep dust from settling on the surfaces. A label on each sleeve identified the exhibit it belonged to: winter sports, town maps, women's institute, seed catalogues, centennial parade. The centennial parade panels were thick because they included mounted photographs. The rack had a wheel lock, and the lock was checked with a foot. It held.

There was a shelf for hardware used in displays. Clear acrylic stands, brass hooks, small wedges, book cradles, and blocks covered in neutral fabric. The book cradles were nested together. The acrylic stands were wrapped in tissue so they would not scratch. One small wedge had slipped behind the others. The attendant moved it forward and made the row even.

At the far end of the room, a dehumidifier stood beside a floor drain. Its collection bucket was empty because it drained through a tube. The tube was clear, and a little water could be seen inside it. The indicator light was green. The attendant wrote "storage dehumidifier normal" on the closing checklist.

Only after this did the storage room lights go off. The door locked. The key returned to the ring with a small, familiar weight. The museum's public rooms had been closed already, but this last check gave the building a more complete stillness. Objects in cases, objects in boxes, objects waiting on the table, and objects not yet chosen for display all rested in their assigned places, held by labels, notes, and the ordinary habits of care.
