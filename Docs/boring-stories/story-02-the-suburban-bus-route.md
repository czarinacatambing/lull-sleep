# The Suburban Bus Route

The number 36 bus began its route at a small turning circle behind the community library. There was no station building, only a shelter with a sloped roof, a bench made of three wooden boards, and a timetable printed behind clear plastic. The plastic was cloudy around the edges, where dust and rain had worked their way underneath, but the middle was easy enough to read. Weekdays were listed in one column, Saturdays in another, Sundays in a third, with the early morning times printed slightly closer together than the afternoon times.

At 6:12, the bus arrived from the depot and paused beside the curb. The driver checked the mirror, adjusted the small fan clipped near the dashboard, and waited until the clock above the fare box changed to 6:14. Then the doors folded closed, the air brakes gave a soft release, and the bus moved away from the library, past the book return slot and the low hedge that separated the parking lot from the sidewalk.

The first stop was Maple and Third. There was a signpost there with a blue route marker attached to it. Behind the signpost stood a mailbox, painted dark green, with a dent near the bottom that had been there for many years. In spring, the grass around the post grew faster than the grass along the rest of the curb, probably because water collected there after rain. The bus stopped even when no one was waiting, because the schedule gave it a little time at the start and there was no reason to hurry.

After Maple and Third, the bus turned onto Belmore Avenue. The houses there were set back from the road, each with a driveway, each driveway with a different arrangement of bins, bicycles, or flowerpots near the garage. A white van was parked halfway under a carport. A basketball hoop leaned over a driveway with no net attached. A row of small solar garden lights lined one path, though in the morning they were unlit and looked like short black pegs.

The second stop was opposite a bakery that opened at seven. At this hour the windows were still dark, but a light was on in the back. Trays could sometimes be seen moving past the narrow kitchen window. The bus shelter there had an advertisement for a dental clinic. The faces in the advertisement were smiling with calm confidence, and the phone number ended in four zeros, which made it easy to remember.

The bus continued past a row of townhouses, all built from the same tan brick. The front doors were different colors, likely chosen later by the owners. One was red, one was deep blue, two were black, and one was a muted green that matched the leaves of the shrubs near the steps. On garbage collection days, the bins stood in a tidy line near the curb, each lid closed, each handle facing toward the house.

At Larch Crescent, the bus slowed for a speed bump. The driver always took the speed bump gently, not because it was large, but because the cup holder near the dashboard was shallow and a paper cup of coffee could shift if the bus moved too sharply. The road curved around a small park with two swings, a slide, and a metal climbing frame shaped like a low dome. The swings moved sometimes when there was wind. On still mornings they hung straight down.

The fourth stop was beside the park. The schedule called it Larch Park West, though there was no sign saying west anywhere nearby. The stop had a narrow concrete pad and no bench. Someone had placed a smooth stone at the base of the post. It was oval and gray, with one pale stripe through it. The stone was not official, but it remained there through winter and into the next summer.

From the park, the bus crossed a small bridge over a drainage channel. The water in the channel was rarely deep. In dry weeks it was only a thin line moving between stones. A metal ruler was bolted to one side of the concrete wall to show water height. The numbers were black, the lines red, and the lower marks were stained from ordinary weather.

The route then passed the elementary school. The bus did not turn into the school loop. It stayed on the main road and stopped at the corner, near a crossing sign. The school yard had painted lines on the asphalt for games: four square, hopscotch, a faded map, and a large circle divided into sections. The map had once shown provinces or states, but the paint had worn away at the borders, leaving shapes that were still orderly but less specific.

At the next intersection, the bus waited at a long light. Across the road stood a gas station with a convenience store attached. The price sign used red digital numbers. The windshield squeegees rested in blue containers between the pumps. A stack of firewood sat beside the store entrance, wrapped in orange mesh bags, even in months when no one was buying firewood. The bus indicator clicked quietly. When the light changed, the bus turned right.

The route entered a wider commercial street. There were more stops here, and the bus moved in a pattern of starting, stopping, opening doors, closing doors, and starting again. A pharmacy. A grocery store. A bank with a drive-through window. A row of small offices with tinted glass. The names on the doors were practical names: accounting, physiotherapy, insurance, alterations. In one window, a sign said back at 1:30, though the sign had been in the same position for several days.

Past the commercial street, the bus entered the older part of the suburb. The trees were taller there, and their branches met over the road in some places. The sidewalks had more cracks, but the cracks had been filled with dark sealant that curved along the pavement like careful handwriting. The bus passed a church with a noticeboard outside. The noticeboard listed a rummage sale from the previous weekend, a choir practice on Thursday, and office hours on Tuesday morning.

At the final residential stop, the bus paused for two minutes because it was ahead of schedule. The driver opened the front door for fresh air. A sprinkler clicked in a nearby yard, rotating slowly from left to right and back again. Water darkened the edge of the sidewalk and gathered in the seam between two concrete slabs. No one boarded. No one got off. The doors closed again when the minute changed.

The last part of the route followed an access road behind a shopping center. Delivery doors lined one side, each with a number stenciled above it. Door 5 had a yellow bollard in front. Door 6 had two pallets stacked beside it. Door 7 had a broom leaning against the wall. The bus did not stop there. It continued to the transit exchange, where several routes met in a wide paved loop.

At the exchange, the number 36 took its place at bay C. The driver set the brake, opened the doors, and checked the printed schedule clipped to the side panel. The next departure was in nine minutes. The bus was quiet. The seats, patterned in blue and gray, held the shape of no one. Outside, the sign for bay C stood still in the morning light, ready to be read by whoever came next.

During the pause at bay C, the driver completed the small tasks that belonged between loops. The transfer slips were straightened in their holder. The route paddle in the front window was checked to make sure it still read 36 and not 36 short turn. A small piece of paper, no larger than a receipt, was picked up from the aisle near the second row. It had no writing on it. The driver placed it in the waste bag beside the seat.

Another bus pulled into bay A, waited for two passengers, and left. A third bus passed behind the exchange without stopping, going out of service toward the depot. The number 36 remained at bay C. The clock above the shelter showed the same time as the clock on the fare box, which was satisfying in a minor way. At the far edge of the exchange, a maintenance worker replaced a poster in a frame. The old poster had advertised a route change that was already complete. The new poster showed holiday service hours in a blue table.

At the next departure, the number 36 left the exchange by the south exit and began the route in reverse. This side of the loop had its own small details. The shopping center loading doors were now on the left. The broom near door 7 had been moved, perhaps by the person who used it. At the bank, the drive-through lane held one car. At the pharmacy, a delivery truck had its hazard lights blinking in a steady rhythm. The bus passed each without comment.

Returning through the older neighborhood, the light had changed. The filled cracks in the sidewalk were less glossy now. A person stood by the church noticeboard reading the rummage sale notice even though the sale had passed. The bus slowed at the stop but did not need to open its doors. A dog watched from behind a storm door, its face visible through the lower pane. It did not bark.

At the school corner, cones had been placed near the curb for a delivery. The bus stopped slightly ahead of its usual position. The crossing sign cast a short shadow. In the yard, no children were outside yet, but a custodian pushed a wheeled bin along the edge of the building. The painted games on the asphalt looked more faded in the brighter light.

The drainage channel under the small bridge held the same thin line of water. From this direction, the metal ruler was easier to see. The lower numbers were stained, the upper numbers clean. A leaf had caught against one stone and trembled in the current without moving downstream. The bus crossed the bridge with a mild change in sound as the tires passed from road to concrete and back to road.

At Larch Park West, two people waited. One carried a folded newspaper. The other carried a reusable shopping bag with a green vegetable printed on the side. They boarded, tapped cards, and chose seats several rows apart. The bus moved on. The swings in the park were still. The sprinkler near the final residential stop had shut off, leaving darker grass in a semicircle. A wet line remained at the edge of the sidewalk.

By the time the bus reached the bakery stop again, the bakery was open. The front lights were on. A small sign in the window said coffee. The advertisement for the dental clinic looked exactly as it had earlier, unchanged by the opening of the bakery or the movement of the morning. The bus stopped, opened its doors, closed them, and continued, keeping to the timetable with the quiet patience of a route that would be driven again and again.
