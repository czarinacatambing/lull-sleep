# The Harbor Noticeboards

The harbor had four public noticeboards, and every Thursday morning someone from the harbor office walked the length of the waterfront to check them. The walk was not difficult. It began at the office beside the fuel dock, continued past the visitor berths, followed the railings by the small boat ramp, crossed the paved area near the fish market, and ended at the board outside the sailing club. The whole route could be done in fifteen minutes if nothing needed changing, but it usually took longer because paper had a way of curling, fading, loosening, or being covered by newer paper.

The first noticeboard was mounted on the outer wall of the harbor office. It had a glass front with a rubber seal and a small lock at the bottom. Inside were the official notices: tide tables, harbor fees, waste disposal rules, emergency phone numbers, and a map showing speed limits inside the breakwater. The tide table was replaced monthly. The fees were replaced annually. The emergency numbers were checked every week even though they rarely changed.

The harbor assistant unlocked the glass and lifted it upward. The hinge squeaked near the end of its travel. A small metal arm held the glass open. The assistant removed one notice about dredging work that had finished the previous week. Four thumbtack holes remained in the cork, a neat rectangle. A new notice went in its place, announcing that the eastern pump-out station would be serviced on Monday morning. The notice was printed on white paper, centered, with the date in bold. It was pinned at all four corners.

The tide table had begun to bow outward slightly. The assistant pressed it flat and added one clear pushpin in the middle of the top edge. The paper settled. The glass was lowered and locked. Reflections of masts and sky appeared on the surface, but the notices remained readable if a person stood close enough.

The second board was near the visitor berths. This board was less formal. It had local taxi numbers, laundromat directions, a map to the grocery store, a weather forecast, and a handwritten card offering bicycle rentals. The card had been there for two summers and was laminated with tape. Its corners were rounded from weather. The phone number was still clear.

At this board, the assistant replaced the weekly forecast. The old sheet had Tuesday through Sunday. The new sheet had Thursday through Wednesday. Wind direction, wind speed, chance of rain, and general conditions. The forecast was printed from the office computer and trimmed with scissors so it would fit below the map. The scissors did not cut perfectly straight, but the edge was close enough. The old sheet went into a folder marked recycling.

The visitor map had a small sticker that said "you are here." The sticker had drifted slightly because the adhesive softened in sun. The assistant peeled it up, placed it back in the right spot, and pressed the edges down with a fingernail. The laundromat directions were still accurate: left at the chandlery, right at the bakery, across from the pharmacy. The bakery had changed its awning from green to blue, but the direction still worked because the sign was clear.

Between the second and third boards, the walkway passed a row of mooring lines. Some lines were coiled on deck. Some crossed from boats to cleats in clean diagonal lines. A few fenders hung between hulls and dock, white or blue, with scuff marks from ordinary use. The water moved quietly against the pilings. A gull stood on a post and looked toward the ramp without much interest.

The third board was beside the small boat ramp. It contained ramp rules, trailer parking instructions, invasive species cleaning guidance, and a laminated drawing of how to tie a bowline. The bowline drawing was not strictly necessary, but people liked it. The rope in the drawing was colored blue, and the path of the knot was shown in numbered steps. Step three had a small arrow that was beginning to fade.

The assistant checked the ramp rules for damage. The lower left corner had pulled loose. Salt air and damp mornings were hard on tape. The assistant removed the old tape and replaced it with two new pieces, one vertical and one horizontal. The paper lay flat again. A notice about a lost oar was removed because the oar had been found. The oar had been left beside the market building, and someone had returned it to the office on Tuesday.

The cleaning guidance remained in good condition. It showed a boat hull, a drain plug, and a bucket. The main instruction was to drain, clean, and dry. The assistant wiped a small streak from the laminated surface with a cloth kept in the side pocket of the clipboard. The streak came away easily. The cloth was folded and returned to the pocket.

Near the fish market, the harbor was busier. A delivery van backed slowly toward a loading door. The driver looked in both mirrors. A worker in a blue apron carried two empty crates outside and stacked them beside a hose. The smell of ice, salt, and diesel mixed with the smell of coffee from the kiosk nearby. The assistant paused at the edge of the paved area until the van had finished reversing, then crossed behind it at a comfortable distance.

The fourth board stood outside the sailing club. It had race schedules, junior lessons, a notice about life jacket checks, and several social announcements. This board belonged to the club, but the harbor office checked it because visitors often read it. The race schedule had been updated by someone else. The dates were written neatly in black marker on a printed grid. A small magnet shaped like an anchor held the top right corner.

The junior lessons notice had lost one pin. It hung by three corners, with the missing corner lifted slightly. The assistant found a spare pin in the clipboard case and pressed it into place. The notice settled into a rectangle again. The social announcements were less tidy. A barbecue sign overlapped a crew-wanted note by half an inch. A reminder about dues covered the bottom of a poster for a safety evening. The assistant did not rearrange these because the club had its own preferences, but made a note that the board was full.

From the sailing club, the assistant looked back along the harbor. The four boards were not visible all at once, but their positions were known. Office wall. Visitor berths. Boat ramp. Sailing club. Each held its portion of ordinary information. Times, numbers, routes, rules, reminders. Each had paper under glass or paper under pins, kept useful by small weekly attention.

On the walk back, the assistant carried the removed notices in the recycling folder. The dredging notice, the old weather sheet, and the found-oar card lay together. At the office, they were placed in the paper bin beside the copier. The clipboard was returned to its hook. The spare pins were counted. The key went back into the drawer.

Outside, the glass of the first noticeboard reflected the fuel dock. Inside it, the new pump-out notice sat flat and centered. The tide table was held by its extra pin. The emergency numbers remained the same. A person passing by could stop, read, nod once, and continue on with the particular comfort of having found the information exactly where it was supposed to be.

The harbor office kept duplicate copies of the most important notices in a drawer beneath the radio desk. The drawer was shallow and a little stiff in damp weather. Inside were folders labeled tides, maps, services, safety, fees, and temporary. The assistant placed the remaining copies of the pump-out notice into the temporary folder. The folder already held a notice about a buoy inspection, a reminder about winter berth applications, and a half sheet explaining where to leave used oil filters.

Beside the folders was a small box of board supplies. Pushpins, magnets, clear tape, two dry cloths, a spare rubber seal for the office board, and a pencil sharpener. The pushpins were divided into colors because the office preferred official notices to use silver pins and informal notices to use colored ones. This was not written anywhere, but it made the boards easier to read. The assistant counted the silver pins and added six from a new packet.

The tide table for next month had not yet been printed, but the file was ready on the office computer. The assistant opened it to check the margins. The high tides were bold, the low tides regular, and the daylight saving note appeared at the bottom in small type. The table fitted on one page if printed at ninety-four percent. At one hundred percent it cut off the final line. A sticky note on the monitor said "tides 94 percent," which had prevented the same mistake for three months.

The harbor master came in briefly and asked whether the sailing club board was full. The assistant said yes, but still readable. The harbor master nodded and wrote "club board crowded" in a notebook. The notebook contained many such observations: ramp sign faded, visitor map okay, gull waste on rail by berth 12, call electrician, fuel hose inspection due. Some were crossed out. Some waited.

After lunch, the assistant walked out again with no clipboard, only to look from a visitor's point of view. The office board could be read from the path if there was no glare. The visitor board required standing close. The ramp board was clear from the parking area. The sailing club board was indeed crowded, but the race schedule remained visible. A person with a question would still find the answer, or at least enough of one to know where to ask.

The afternoon light shifted across the harbor. The glass fronts of the boards reflected masts, ropes, passing clouds, and the slow movement of water. Behind the reflections, the papers stayed still. Their job was modest: to say what time, how much, which way, who to call, where to put things, and when something would be temporarily unavailable. The harbor relied on engines, lines, charts, weather, fuel, and skill, but it also relied on paper under glass, checked every Thursday.
