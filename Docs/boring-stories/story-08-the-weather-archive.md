# The Weather Archive

The weather archive occupied two rooms on the third floor of the county administration building. It was not a public office, though visitors could request records at the counter downstairs. The archive rooms were reached by a service elevator and a short hallway with framed photographs of storms, river floods, clear winter mornings, and one unusually large hailstone placed beside a ruler. The photographs had been there for many years, and the captions beneath them were printed on cream paper.

The first room held paper records. The second held equipment, duplicate binders, and a computer station used for scanning. The paper records were arranged by year, then by station, then by month. Some stations had complete records going back decades. Others had gaps. A gap could mean a station had closed, moved, lost power, changed observers, or simply misplaced a form. The archive did not judge the gaps. It recorded them.

On Tuesday mornings, the archive clerk processed the previous week's observation sheets. The sheets arrived in envelopes from small stations around the county. Some were mailed. Some were dropped off by public works staff. A few came by email and were printed because the archive still kept paper copies. Each sheet listed date, high temperature, low temperature, precipitation, snow depth if applicable, wind notes if observed, and comments.

The clerk opened the first envelope with a dull silver letter opener. The envelope was from Station 12, located near the reservoir. Inside were seven sheets, one for each day. The handwriting was careful and upright. Monday high: 68. Monday low: 47. Rain: trace. Comments: light fog before 0700. The clerk checked that all seven days were present, then placed the sheets in order.

Trace rainfall had its own notation. It meant precipitation was observed but too little to measure. The archive used a capital T in the rainfall column. The clerk checked that the station observer had used T and not zero. A zero meant no precipitation. A T meant a little, but not enough. Small distinctions mattered because records accumulated slowly, and later someone might ask a question that depended on them.

The next envelope came from Station 4, at the airport maintenance yard. These sheets were typed. The airport station included wind direction at noon and visibility notes during unusual conditions. Tuesday listed a north wind at twelve miles per hour. Wednesday listed calm. Thursday had a note about haze. The clerk entered the data into the computer database, then initialed the bottom right corner of each sheet.

After entry, the sheets were scanned. The scanner sat on a table by itself. It had a hinged lid, a feeder tray, and a small display that said ready. The clerk placed the sheets face up in the feeder, adjusted the guides, and pressed the green button. The scanner pulled each page through with a steady sound. On the computer screen, thumbnails appeared one by one. If a page was crooked, it was rescanned. If a page was faint, the contrast setting was adjusted.

Station 9 sent forms on blue paper. No one remembered why. The blue paper scanned well enough, but the clerk always checked those images more carefully because pencil marks could fade against the background. Station 9 was a volunteer station near the old mill road. Its observer added comments more often than most. Robins in yard. Frost on bridge. Barometer falling. Smoke from west field. The official fields were numeric, but the comments gave the sheets a quiet sense of place.

Once scanned, each week's sheets were placed in acid-free folders. The folders were labeled with year, station, and week number. Week numbers had caused confusion in the past, so the archive used the county system printed on a reference calendar. The calendar hung beside the filing table. Each week was outlined in gray. Holidays were marked in small red type but did not change the numbering.

At eleven, the clerk answered a request from downstairs. A visitor wanted rainfall totals for June of a year sixteen years earlier. The clerk found the year on the shelf, pulled the station binder, and turned to June. The binder rings opened with a stiff click. The rainfall column was totaled first by hand, then checked on the calculator. The calculator had large buttons and printed no paper tape. The total was written on a request slip and sent downstairs.

The archive also kept old instruments that were no longer used. In the second room, a glass thermometer hung in a wooden case. A rain gauge stood beside it, made of metal with a funnel top. There was a wind vane from a school roof, a barograph with a paper chart wrapped around a drum, and several small boxes of replacement chart paper. The barograph had not been wound in years, but its pen arm still rested delicately against the chart.

After lunch, the clerk checked the digital files. Each scan had to match its paper folder. The file names followed a pattern: year, station, week. If a station number had only one digit, it received a leading zero. Station 4 became station 04. This kept the folders sorted correctly on the computer. The clerk found one file named station4 without the zero and renamed it.

The database had a validation report. It listed missing values, unlikely values, and entries outside expected ranges. Most warnings were harmless. A high temperature of 86 in October might be unusual but possible. A low temperature higher than the high temperature was probably a transposition. A rainfall amount of 99.00 was likely a placeholder that had not been corrected. The clerk reviewed each warning against the original sheet.

One warning appeared for Station 12: precipitation missing on Sunday. The paper sheet showed a blank line. The clerk called the observer, who checked a notebook and confirmed zero. The blank was updated to 0.00. The database warning disappeared. The paper sheet received a small note in the margin: confirmed zero by phone, with date and initials.

By three, the week's records were entered, scanned, filed, and backed up. The clerk returned the binders to the shelves, making sure each spine label faced outward. The room was cool and dry. A small dehumidifier hummed near the corner, its indicator light green. The window blinds were tilted upward to keep direct sun off the shelves.

Before leaving, the clerk looked at the current observation board. It was a whiteboard where today's reported conditions were written before entry. The numbers were ordinary. Mild morning. Light wind. No measurable rain. The board would be wiped tomorrow and filled again. Behind it, the archive held years of similar numbers, each one small by itself, each one kept because weather became history one line at a time.

The archive also maintained a shelf of monthly summaries. These were not raw observations but tidy pages made from them. Each summary listed averages, extremes, totals, and notes. Warmest day. Coolest night. Total precipitation. Number of days with measurable rain. Snowfall, if there was snow. The summaries were printed on heavier paper and placed in binders with blue spines. The blue had faded on the oldest binders to a softer color, somewhere between blue and gray.

When a month ended, the clerk compared the summary to the daily sheets. This was slower than letting the computer do it alone, but it caught small things. A trace counted as a precipitation day but not as a measurable amount. Snow depth could remain after no new snow had fallen. A temperature recorded at midnight could belong to the previous observation period depending on station rules. The archive had notes for these situations in a procedural binder.

The procedural binder was thick and practical. It explained how to handle missing data, station moves, replaced instruments, observer notes, and corrections. A tab marked "examples" contained photocopies of old forms with annotations. One example showed a rain gauge overflow after a storm. Another showed a thermometer reading that was clearly impossible because the decimal had been placed in the wrong position. The examples were not exciting. They were reassuring because they showed that confusing records could be handled patiently.

At the scanning station, the clerk backed up the day's files to an external drive kept in a padded case. The drive was small and black, with a white label showing the backup rotation. There were three drives. One in use, one in the office safe, one stored off site. The clerk connected the cable, waited for the icon to appear, and ran the backup script from a shortcut on the desktop. When the confirmation appeared, the time was written on the backup log.

The final task was to place the empty envelopes in date order. Envelopes were kept for one month in case a question arose about postmarks or station origin. After a month, they were recycled. The envelopes from Station 12, Station 4, and Station 9 went into a shallow box marked current month. Their corners did not line up exactly because the envelopes were different sizes.

As the clerk turned off the lights, the rooms settled into their controlled conditions. Paper in folders, folders in boxes, boxes on shelves. Numbers in binders, scans in files, backups in rotation. Outside, the weather continued without consulting the archive. Inside, the archive waited to receive it later, after someone read a gauge, wrote a number, mailed a sheet, and added one more ordinary day to the long record.
