# The Municipal Greenhouse

The municipal greenhouse stood behind the parks department building, beyond a chain-link gate and a row of gravel parking spaces. From the street, only the roof could be seen, a long ridge of glass panels with aluminum frames that caught the morning light in separate rectangles. The building was used mostly for practical plants: trays of annuals for traffic medians, small shrubs for replacement beds, seedlings for the library courtyard, and a few experimental pots that someone had labeled with wooden sticks.

Inside, the air was warmer than outside and slightly damp. A narrow concrete path ran down the center. Benches stood on both sides, each bench made of metal mesh on a frame. Water could pass through the mesh, and fallen soil collected beneath it in soft dark lines. Along the left side were the young plants. Along the right side were the older plants, waiting to be moved out when the ground was ready.

The first bench held marigolds. There were six trays, each tray divided into small square cells. The leaves were fine and feathery, the flower buds still closed. A white plastic label at the end of the bench read "marigold, orange, boulevard east." The label had been written in permanent marker, though the word boulevard had faded more than the others. Beside the marigolds were petunias, then alyssum, then a flat of dusty miller with soft gray leaves that looked as if they had collected powder.

The greenhouse supervisor carried a clipboard with a watering sheet. The sheet was divided into zones. Zone one: seedlings. Zone two: hanging baskets. Zone three: shrubs. Zone four: holding area. Each zone had boxes for morning and afternoon, and beside each box was a space for initials. On rainy days, the outdoor holding area was skipped. On very hot days, some plants were checked three times.

Watering began with the smallest seedlings. The hose was fitted with a gentle wand so the water came out in a fine shower instead of a hard stream. The supervisor moved slowly, pausing above each tray until the soil darkened evenly. The water made a soft tapping sound on leaves and mesh. Some drops gathered on the edges of the trays before falling through to the floor. The hose followed in a loose curve behind, then straightened as it was pulled forward.

The hanging baskets were suspended from rails overhead. Each basket had a hook, a chain, a plastic pot, and a small tag tucked into the soil. Some baskets held trailing petunias. Some held ivy geraniums. Some held a mixture that would look fuller later, after more growth. For now the plants were modest, with stems reaching outward as if considering the space around them. A few leaves touched the chains.

At the back of the greenhouse was the potting area. There was a large worktable with a soil bin underneath. The soil bin had a sliding lid that never slid smoothly, because grains of soil settled in the track. On the table were stacks of empty pots sorted by size. Four-inch pots, six-inch pots, one-gallon pots. The four-inch pots were black and flexible. The six-inch pots were green. The one-gallon pots were reused from previous seasons and had old labels scraped from their sides.

A bag of perlite leaned against the wall, light enough to shift when brushed by a sleeve. A bag of compost sat beside it, heavier and flatter. There were scoops in a bucket: one large metal scoop, one cracked plastic scoop, and one small scoop made from a cut-down detergent bottle. The detergent-bottle scoop was useful for filling narrow pots, so it remained in use even though it was not official equipment.

On a shelf above the potting table were jars of plant ties, bamboo stakes, twist ties, spare labels, and pencils. The pencils were kept in a mug from a local radio station. Most were short. One had been sharpened so many times that it was barely longer than the eraser. The labels were bundled with rubber bands, and the rubber bands were beginning to dry out, so the supervisor made a note to order more.

The experimental pots occupied a sunny corner. They were not part of the seasonal planting plan. They held cuttings from a shrub near city hall, a tray of herbs someone hoped might survive in the plaza planters, and three small pots labeled "unknown sedge." The sedge had come in mixed with other material and was being observed. It grew upright in thin green blades, not dramatic but steady. The supervisor measured one blade with a ruler and wrote four and a half inches on the tag.

At midmorning, another worker arrived with a cart. The cart had two shelves and one wheel that squeaked only when turned left. Together they loaded geraniums for the senior center beds. The order sheet called for red, white, and salmon, with the salmon placed in the center section. The plants were counted twice. Twelve red. Twelve white. Eight salmon. Two extra red were added in case of damage during planting.

The cart rolled down the center path, through the greenhouse door, and out into the yard. The door closed behind it slowly, because the hydraulic arm was old and cautious. Outside, the plants waited on the cart while the truck was unlocked. Inside, the empty space on the bench was swept with a hand brush, and a new tray was moved forward to fill the gap.

Near noon, the vents opened automatically. There was a small mechanical sound above, then a slight change in the air. The top panels lifted just enough to let warm air drift out. A breeze moved through the greenhouse and stirred the lightest leaves. The hanging baskets swayed almost imperceptibly. The supervisor checked the thermometer near the door and wrote the number on the watering sheet, not because it was required, but because it was useful to know.

Lunch was taken in the parks department break room, but the greenhouse did not become empty. The plants continued their slow work. Water settled into soil. A drop slid from a leaf to the bench below. Condensation gathered along one pane of glass and then cleared as the venting improved. The hose lay coiled near the spigot. The clipboard hung from a nail beside the door.

In the afternoon, the supervisor filled small gaps in the library courtyard order. Three flats of begonias, two flats of coleus, one tray of edging plants with silver leaves. The labels were checked against the plan. The plan was laminated and had been used for several years, with some changes marked in grease pencil. The north bed received shade plants. The south bed received sun plants. The circular planter by the entrance received whatever looked best after the other beds were assigned.

By four, the greenhouse had cooled a little. The watering sheet was complete except for the final holding-area check. The supervisor walked the length of the building one more time, touching a few pots to judge moisture by weight. Some needed water. Most did not. A fallen label was found under the marigold bench and placed back in its tray. The hose was coiled, the vents were checked, and the door was latched.

Outside, the glass roof reflected the late afternoon sky in pale panels. Inside, the rows remained in order: seedlings to the left, older plants to the right, potting table at the back, experimental pots in the corner. Nothing hurried. Nothing needed to happen all at once. The greenhouse kept its warm, quiet inventory of leaves, labels, trays, and water.

There was also a small record book kept in a drawer beneath the potting bench. The book had a green cover, softened at the corners, and pages printed with columns for date, crop, tray number, sowing notes, germination notes, and action needed. It was not beautiful, but it was dependable. The supervisor opened it after the final watering and turned to the pages for early spring. Each tray number matched a plastic tag somewhere in the greenhouse.

Tray 18 had been seeded with snapdragons. The germination note said uneven but acceptable. Tray 22 had coleus cuttings, most rooted. Tray 27 had basil that had stretched too much toward the light before being moved. The supervisor wrote "pinch next week" in the action column. This did not mean anything urgent. It meant the basil would be encouraged to branch instead of growing tall and thin.

The record book also contained reminders that had nothing to do with plants directly. Repair north bench foot. Replace hose washer. Order labels. Check shade cloth. The north bench foot had been repaired with a square of pressure-treated wood, and the note could now be crossed out. The hose washer had been replaced that morning. The old washer, flattened and stiff, lay on the edge of the sink until it was thrown away.

Near the sink, several watering cans stood upside down. They were used for areas where the hose was awkward, especially around the experimental corner. One can was red, one was green, and one was galvanized metal with a removable rose. The rose was the perforated cap that made the water fall softly. Its holes sometimes clogged with minerals, so it was soaked in vinegar in a shallow dish. The dish was labeled so no one would mistake it for something else.

The propagation mat on the back bench was unplugged for the season. In colder weeks it had warmed trays from below, gently and evenly. Now the air was warm enough without it. The cord was wrapped loosely and tied with a twist tie. The plug was kept off the floor. A folded towel covered the mat to keep soil from settling into its surface.

Above the potting area, the automatic vent control had a small dial. The dial was set to the same mark it had been set to the week before. The supervisor checked it anyway. The mark was a notch, not a number, made with a fine permanent marker after someone had found the best setting through trial and adjustment. The greenhouse did not need many clever changes. It needed repeated attention to small conditions.

At the end of the day, the supervisor copied three notes from the record book onto a whiteboard by the door. Move geranium extras. Pinch basil next week. Order medium labels. The whiteboard had faint traces of older writing that could be seen when the light hit it from the side. A black marker rested in the tray. A blue marker had dried out and was placed in the office to be discarded.

When the door was locked, the plants were left with everything they needed for the night: damp soil where damp soil was wanted, open space where air should move, labels beside each tray, and the roof above them holding the last brightness of evening. The greenhouse did not become silent all at once. It kept the tiny sounds of water settling, leaves relaxing, and metal cooling by degrees.
