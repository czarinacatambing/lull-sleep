# The Supply Closet Inventory

There was a supply closet at the end of the second floor hallway, just past the rooms where the afternoon sun usually warmed the upper part of the doors and left the lower part cool. The closet had a small brass number on it, 214B, although no one had ever seemed to know what 214A was. The number was held on by two screws that had been painted over at least once, so the slots were softened and filled with a thin rim of cream-colored paint.

Inside the closet, the shelves were arranged in a way that suggested a person had once made a careful plan and then several other people had followed it closely enough. On the top shelf were the paper goods. Reams of white printer paper sat in two stacks, each stack turned at a right angle to the other so the long sides faced outward on one pile and the short sides faced outward on the other. The wrappers were slightly different shades of blue, because the school had changed suppliers in March, and the older packages had a small picture of a mountain on the corner while the newer ones had only text.

Next to the paper were three packages of pale yellow legal pads. The plastic around them was tight and shiny, and the corners of the cardboard backs made small square impressions in it. One pad had been opened and then placed back on the shelf. It had forty-six sheets remaining, because someone had counted them during the last inventory and written the number in pencil on the cardboard backing, near the top, where it could be seen without lifting the pad.

Below the paper shelf were the writing instruments. The pens were divided by color, mostly. Black pens filled a clear bin with a cracked handle, and blue pens filled a green bin that had once held holiday decorations. The red pens were in a coffee mug that said "room 18," even though room 18 had been renumbered years ago. There were fewer red pens than blue or black, which was ordinary. Most of them had caps, and the ones without caps had been laid sideways in the mug so they would not mark the bottom.

The pencils were in two groups. Sharpened pencils rested point-down in a square metal cup with a felt pad underneath. Unsharpened pencils lay flat in a cardboard tray that had a shallow crease along one side. The pencils had different erasers. Some were pink and firm, some were white and soft, and some were missing entirely, leaving only the silver ferrule with its two pressed bands. A small pencil sharpener sat beside them, the kind with a transparent chamber for shavings. There were shavings in it already, curled like tiny beige ribbons.

The third shelf was for tape, glue, and labels. There were six rolls of clear tape in their plastic dispensers, two rolls of masking tape, and one roll of blue painter's tape that had been used only a little. The edge of the painter's tape was folded under to make a tab. The glue sticks stood upright in a short box, all with the caps facing up. Someone had written "new" on the box, though at least one glue stick had a small smudge near its label, and another had been twisted up once and twisted back down again.

The labels came in several sizes. There were large mailing labels, small return-address labels, round colored dots, and a sheet of rectangular file-folder labels in a shade of green that was almost but not quite mint. The round dots were stored by color in little paper envelopes. Red, yellow, blue, and green were all present. Orange had only four dots left, each one stuck in a neat row near the top of its backing sheet.

On the fourth shelf were the folders. Manila folders came first, then blue folders, then green folders, then a smaller group of purple folders that had been ordered for a project and never fully used. The manila folders were standing vertically in a magazine holder. Their tabs alternated left, center, and right, though the pattern broke near the back, where three center tabs stood together. The blue folders were still wrapped in paper bands, twenty-five per band. One band had slipped halfway down, and its folders leaned slightly to the side.

The bottom shelf held larger objects. There was a box of clipboards, a box of binder clips, a plastic tub of rulers, and a stack of empty three-ring binders. The binders were mostly white, with transparent sleeves on the front and spine. One binder had a sleeve that was a little cloudy from age. Another had a tiny triangle of old paper stuck inside the front cover, as if a title page had once been inserted and then removed in a hurry.

The clipboards were arranged face to face, so the metal clips did not scratch the writing surfaces. Each clip had the same curved shape and the same small hole at the top. The binder clips were sorted by size in three compartments of a tray that had originally been made for utensils. Large clips on the left, medium clips in the center, small clips on the right. The smallest clips were the most numerous. They made a small, tidy pile, black with silver wire handles folded down.

Near the door, hanging from two hooks, were several lanyards. They were not part of the official inventory, because they did not belong clearly to any category. One was red, one was navy, and one had a repeated pattern of tiny white stars. Below them, a dustpan and brush hung from a separate hook. The brush had gray bristles that fanned outward slightly, and the dustpan had a shallow scratch across its handle.

The clipboard for the inventory hung on a string tied to the inner doorknob. The paper on the clipboard had columns for item, quantity, shelf, condition, and notes. The handwriting changed halfway down the page, becoming slightly rounder and more spaced out, because the first person had been called away and the second person had continued the count. The date at the top was written carefully, with the month spelled out.

At the end of the inventory, the closet looked the same as it had before, except perhaps a little more known. The door was closed gently. The latch made a soft click. In the hallway, the light from the far window rested on the floor in a long rectangle, and the small brass number 214B kept its place beside the door, quiet and exact.

The next part of the count, which was not always done but was useful when there was time, involved the smaller drawers of the cabinet against the back wall. The cabinet was beige metal, with six shallow drawers and one deeper drawer at the bottom. Each drawer had a label holder, and each label holder contained a strip of paper cut slightly narrower than the slot. The first drawer was labeled staples. Inside were boxes of standard staples, two partial boxes of heavy-duty staples, and one small stapler without a matching size marked anywhere on it. The small stapler was tested on a scrap of paper. It worked, and so it stayed.

The second drawer held rubber bands. The rubber bands were separated by size in envelopes that had been reused from old mailings. Large bands in one envelope, medium bands in another, small bands in a third. The medium bands were the most useful. A few had dried into flat shapes and were removed, because a rubber band that broke while holding papers together created more work than it saved. The good ones were returned to their envelope, and the flap was tucked in without sealing it.

The third drawer contained fasteners. Brass brads, paper clips, pushpins, and two small boxes of map tacks. The brass brads had round heads that caught the light when the drawer was opened. The paper clips were mostly silver, though one blue clip and one striped clip were found among them. The pushpins had plastic heads in mixed colors, and the map tacks were all white. The map tacks were counted twice because they rolled slightly when the box was moved.

The fourth drawer was for envelopes. Small white envelopes, larger manila envelopes, a few padded envelopes, and several interoffice envelopes with rows of names crossed out. The interoffice envelopes had strings that looped around cardboard circles. Some strings were frayed, but most were usable. The padded envelopes were not perfectly flat because their bubble lining held a little air. They were stacked with the openings all facing the same direction.

The fifth drawer held miscellaneous labels that had lost their original packaging. There were file tabs, blank stickers, binder spine inserts, and a few adhesive pockets for name cards. The spine inserts were curled from being stored too long. They were placed under the legal pads for a while to flatten. This was noted in the margin of the inventory sheet, not because it mattered much, but because someone opening the drawer later might wonder where they had gone.

The sixth drawer was nearly empty. It contained a roll of string, two replacement blades for a paper cutter, and a small sealed package of felt pads for chair legs. None of these belonged exactly in the supply closet, but all of them were things someone might reasonably look for there. They were counted as miscellaneous and left in place.

The deep bottom drawer held seasonal items: extra sign holders, laminated arrows, blank door tags, and a stack of unused visitor badges. The visitor badges were attached to clips. The clips were tested by opening and closing three of them at random. All three worked. The laminated arrows pointed left or right depending on how they were turned, which made them more flexible than arrows printed with words. They were stacked together and returned to the drawer.

When the cabinet was done, the inventory sheet had a second page. The paper curled slightly where it had been held on the clipboard. The pencil numbers were neat enough. Nothing urgent had been discovered. A few things needed ordering eventually, but not immediately: orange dots, medium rubber bands, perhaps one box of blue pens before the autumn. The closet remained a closet, but now its contents had been asked a quiet question and had answered in quantities, conditions, and notes.
