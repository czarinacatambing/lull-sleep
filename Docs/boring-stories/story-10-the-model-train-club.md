# The Model Train Club

The model train club met in a room at the back of the community center, past the pottery studio and across from the storage closet where folding chairs were kept. The room had no windows, but it had bright overhead lights and a large layout built on wooden tables that joined together around the walls. In the middle was an open space where members could stand, lean, adjust, discuss, and occasionally search for a tiny part that had fallen from a bridge or signal post.

The layout represented no particular town, though it borrowed features from several. There was a station with two platforms, a freight yard, a small industrial area, a hill with a tunnel, a row of houses, a river crossing, a farm, a road bridge, and a section of forest made from many small trees. The trees were different heights and shades of green. Some had trunks visible. Some were only textured clumps on wire stems. From a distance, the forest looked continuous. Up close, each tree had its own little position.

On Thursday evening, the first member arrived at six-thirty and unlocked the cabinet where the controllers were stored. The controllers were labeled with masking tape: main line, branch line, yard, test track. The tape on the main line controller had been replaced recently and was still bright. The test track label was older and had a fingerprint near one edge.

The room log lay on a clipboard near the door. Members signed in with name, time, and task. The tasks were usually brief. Clean track. Ballast west curve. Repair fence. Test engine 42. Paint crates. Sort figures. The first member wrote "check station lights" and placed the pen back under the clip.

Station lights were small, warm, and sometimes unreliable. They ran on thin wires beneath the platform. The member switched on the layout power and watched the station. Most lights came on at once. One lamp near the ticket office flickered, went dark, returned, and then held steady. The member made a note to check the solder joint later, but not immediately. A flicker that settled was not urgent.

At six-forty, two more members arrived with a cardboard box of scenery supplies. The box contained ground foam, spare track nails, a bottle of scenic cement, a bag of miniature bushes, three paintbrushes, and a packet of tiny people. The tiny people were unpainted, molded in pale plastic. Some were standing. Some were seated. One appeared to be carrying a suitcase. They would eventually be painted and placed on the platforms, sidewalks, or park benches.

The evening's main task was the west curve, where the ballast looked too even. Real ballast, someone explained, did not lie like cake frosting. It had variation, small gaps, shoulders, and places where maintenance had changed the texture. The model ballast was made from fine gray stone. A member used a small spoon to add a little more along the outside of the curve. Another used a dry brush to shape it away from the rails. The work was slow because no stone should sit against the inner side of the rail where wheels would pass.

Once the ballast was shaped, it was misted with water from a spray bottle. The spray bottle had to be held high enough that droplets fell gently. Then scenic cement was applied with a pipette, drop by drop. The liquid darkened the ballast at first. Later it would dry clear. The members leaned close but did not touch the wet section. A small paper sign was placed beside it: wet glue.

At the freight yard, another member tested engine 42. It was a blue diesel with a slightly uneven coupler height. The engine moved forward on the test track, stopped, reversed, stopped again, and then moved slowly through a turnout. The wheels clicked softly over the points. The member watched from track level, one eye close to the table, checking whether the coupler dipped. It did, but only a little. A thin washer might fix it. The engine was placed in a foam cradle for later adjustment.

The houses near the row of shops had recently received porch railings. The railings were delicate and white. One had come loose at the corner. A member applied glue with the end of a toothpick and held the railing in place for thirty seconds. The house itself was yellow with a gray roof, green shutters, and a chimney made from a separate piece of molded plastic. Behind it was a garden patch with rows of dark soil and no plants yet.

At seven-fifteen, the club president made tea in the small kitchen next door. The kettle took several minutes. While it heated, members continued working in the quiet way of people who had done similar tasks many times. A brush moved along a road edge. A box of telephone poles was counted. The river surface was dusted with a soft cloth. The tunnel portal was checked for clearance by passing a tall freight car through it slowly.

The tea arrived in mismatched mugs. Mugs were placed away from the layout on a side table, because a spill could damage scenery. There was also a plate of plain biscuits. Someone took one biscuit, then another half. Crumbs were brushed into a napkin. The conversation stayed practical: whether the farm lane needed more gravel, whether the station roof should be weathered, whether the club should order more static grass before the exhibition in autumn.

After tea, the tiny people were sorted. Standing figures in one tray. Seated figures in another. Workers, passengers, children, cyclists, and uncertain figures whose purpose could be decided later. One seated figure had a hat. Another appeared to hold a newspaper. A standing figure with one arm raised might be waving, signaling, or pointing toward something. The sorter wrote "paint batch A" on a small envelope and placed ten figures inside.

The branch line was run for a short test. A small green locomotive pulled two coaches from the station, around the west curve, over the river bridge, and toward the tunnel. It moved slowly because the ballast glue was wet near the main line and no one wanted vibration to disturb it. The coaches had cream window frames. Their wheels made a gentle, even sound over the rail joints. At the tunnel, the locomotive entered darkness and emerged on the other side after a few seconds.

Near eight-thirty, cleanup began. Brushes were rinsed. Glue caps were tightened. The spray bottle was emptied halfway so pressure would not build. Loose ground foam was returned to its container. The wet glue sign remained in place. Engine 42 stayed in its foam cradle with a note about the washer. The station lights were switched off, then on again, then off. The lamp near the ticket office did not flicker the second time.

The room log was updated before everyone left. West curve ballasted, wet. Engine 42 coupler needs washer. Porch railing repaired. Figures sorted batch A. Station light observe next week. The president turned off the layout power. The room became less like a town and more like a room again, but only slightly. The station, bridge, forest, houses, farm, and quiet track remained in place, waiting under the overhead lights for the next Thursday evening.

Before the lights were turned off, one member checked the storage drawers beneath the layout. Each drawer had a small brass pull and a label. Track parts. Road vehicles. Building spares. Trees. Paint. Figures. Electrical. The electrical drawer was opened only halfway because the wires inside had a tendency to catch on the back. It contained switches, connectors, small bulbs, heat-shrink tubing, and a packet of resistors in paper strips. The packet was returned to the front so it would be visible next time.

The road vehicles drawer held cars, vans, trucks, and one bus. Some were finished and ready to place. Some still needed license plates or a wash of thin paint to make them look less new. The bus was cream and red. It had clear windows and no passengers. Someone suggested placing it near the station, but the road there was still being planned. The bus went back into the drawer, wrapped loosely in tissue.

The trees drawer was fuller than expected. There were tall pines, small deciduous trees, bare winter trees that had not been used, and several homemade armatures twisted from fine wire. A note in the drawer said "do not buy more trees yet." The note was underlined twice. The club had bought trees three times because everyone forgot how many were already stored. The note was left on top.

At the farm section, the fence line was inspected. Fence posts leaned slightly by design, but one leaned because it had come unstuck. This was written in the log for another week. The farm lane also needed a little more dust-colored powder near the gate. The farmhouse porch, however, looked right. A tiny rocking chair sat beside the door, and a milk can stood near the steps. Neither needed adjustment.

The bridge over the river received one last look. The river was made from poured gloss medium, layered over paint. In the center it was darker. Near the banks it was greenish brown. A canoe had been placed by the reeds, tied to a post with thread. The thread was almost invisible unless the light caught it. The member checking the bridge made sure no dust had settled in the water surface. None had.

When the overhead lights finally went off, the layout disappeared by sections. First the forest, then the farm, then the bridge, then the station platforms, until only the exit sign by the door remained visible. The members left through the community center hallway, passing the pottery studio and the chair closet. Behind them, the model town held its shape in the dark, with trains on sidings, wet ballast drying on the west curve, and tiny unpainted passengers waiting in an envelope marked batch A.
